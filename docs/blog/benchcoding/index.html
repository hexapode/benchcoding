A continuing search, with someone responsible for it

Benchcoding puts an engineer in charge of a continuing search for improvements to a measurable outcome. The engineer maintains a shared view of the experiments, decides where to spend the next round of effort, and reviews what survives evaluation. Other people can contribute without waiting for an individual task.

A capable engineer already asks agents to investigate bottlenecks, propose alternatives, and test them against benchmarks. That is the comparison this proposal has to beat. Giving an agent an outcome is familiar practice; the additional commitment here is to keep the search useful across successive rounds and, eventually, across teams.

The reason to try this now is an economic hypothesis: agents may reduce the cost of testing an idea enough to make a wider search worthwhile. If so, evaluating candidates and choosing the next experiments will occupy more of the engineer’s time. That benefit disappears if review, duplicate attempts, or evaluation hardware absorb the savings.

There are two hypotheses to test. First, maintaining this search within one team improves the return on its experiments. Second, opening it to other teams adds enough useful ideas to cover the extra coordination. The first can succeed while the second fails. A team already doing all of this may find the name describes its existing practice.

What the board helps you discover

Take a parser whose large tables and scanned invoices are slow. The owner fixes a representative workload and a baseline, then asks agents to reduce processing time without breaking accuracy or memory limits. The board records what is being tried and why, linking each attempt to its code, evaluation version, and cost.

In this fictional example, the owner expects the table detector to be the bottleneck. An agent tracing the pipeline instead finds that two stages decode and resize the same image separately. Passing the prepared image between them avoids repeated work without changing the detector. That becomes the “Reuse preprocessing” candidate below: an approach the owner hadn’t thought to assign.

A normal open-ended investigation could find the same thing. The board’s proposed benefit comes in the next round: another contributor can see what was discovered, avoid repeating failed cache variants, and test whether the shared intermediate helps other document types. The owner can fund that question instead of commissioning another broad investigation from scratch.

What does the review find?

The fastest submission omits difficult pages. Reusing preprocessing still produces the expected output when replayed on held-out documents, so it can go forward for integration review. Batching table detection runs faster but exceeds the memory limit; its author would need to fix that before resubmitting.

The next decision is to test reuse on the expensive document types before integrating it. The batching approach stays on the board with its memory failure attached, available if someone has a specific way to fix it. As changes enter the baseline, the owner closes obsolete experiments so contributors aren’t optimizing an old version.

What another team adds

The invoice-search team has a reason to contribute: parsing delays its own product. It sees the shared-intermediate result and recognizes a related cache it already maintains. Using its experiment allowance, it tests whether that experience transfers to the parser. The parser owner decides whether to pay for follow-up work; each entry records whose budget funded it.

Participation requires time and permission, even when agents do much of the implementation. Contributors receive credit for accepted work, while the parser team agrees to maintain the resulting code and handle its on-call consequences. A dependency that team cannot support is a reason to decline a change despite its benchmark result.

The owner delegates routine reviews to teammates and asks contributors for replayable submissions. When the queue grows faster than the team can assess it, new experiments pause. Coordination has a cost, which belongs in the comparison with keeping the search inside one team.

Reading agent traces remains part of the job. Repeated edits to a failing assertion deserve investigation, as do several runs pursuing the same unsuccessful approach. The owner uses that evidence to change assignments or repair the evaluation, sometimes by writing the diagnostic code personally.

Owning the leaderboard means being accountable for what its numbers mean.

Public examples, an internal hypothesis

Big O Speedrun tracks attempts to improve mathematical bounds, distinguishing Claimed, Human Verified, and Lean Verified results. Its Lean verification process checks the proof against the intended problem statement. Contributors can inspect both the current result and the evidence behind it. [1]

The Open Kernel Challenge gives entrants a common GPU optimization target, with fresh-input correctness checks and timing against SGLang on the same hardware. Its serving track uses hidden conversation workloads. [2]

These public competitions suggest a way to make problems available to contributors. They do not establish that an internal board outperforms a well-run engineering team. That is what the pilot needs to determine.

Evaluating what comes back

A submission can exploit the evaluation by skipping inputs, relaxing tolerances, or moving expensive work outside the timer. “Cheating” describes that behavior here, without assuming anything about an agent’s intentions. Reviewers need to inspect the measured code path and reproduce the result from the submitted revision.

Keep the evaluator outside the agent’s writable workspace and reserve a separate acceptance workload for promising candidates. Repeated feedback leaks information about hidden tests, so limit those queries and refresh the workload deliberately. When evaluation changes, rerun the baseline and affected candidates rather than ranking incompatible scores together.

The parser owner remains responsible for whether the workload represents customer traffic. Changes must also work together: two successful candidates may eliminate the same repeated computation or depend on conflicting assumptions. Integration tests and a limited production rollout determine whether the benefit survives beyond an isolated run.

Test the two hypotheses separately

Start with one team and a component it understands. Before any runs, fix the workload, acceptance constraints, budget, and the cost assigned to engineer time. Agree on the minimum improvement that would justify continuing.

First: does maintaining the search help?

Compare Benchcoding within the team against a capable engineer conducting an open-ended agent investigation with their usual tools. Both can explore alternatives, run agents in parallel, and follow promising results. Use the same starting code and comparable compute and human-time allowances. Run several matched trials, alternating which workflow gets each problem, rather than relying on one discovery.

For the parser, define the return as milliseconds of accepted median processing-time reduction per dollar of total cost, measured on the fixed workload under unchanged accuracy and memory constraints. From 820 ms to an accepted 670 ms, the numerator is 150 ms. Count failed runs, evaluation hardware, setup, review, and integration in the denominator; assign zero accepted reduction when no improvement survives. Report absolute latency and total cost alongside the ratio.

One provisional continuation rule is at least 10% lower processing time and 20% more accepted reduction per total dollar than the comparison workflow, with maintenance accepted by the team and the gain confirmed in a limited rollout. These are suggested pilot thresholds to agree or replace beforehand, not observed results. Show the variation across trials; an uncertain difference calls for more evidence.

Then: does cross-team participation pay for itself?

If the first stage earns another trial, compare the within-team process with the same process opened to other teams. Start from equivalent fresh baselines and keep total compute and engineer-time allowances comparable, including contributors’ time. Otherwise, adding more people and budget would confound the result.

Use the same return measure and choose an additional margin before starting; for example, require another 20% improvement in accepted reduction per total dollar after coordination costs. Track whether outside contributions introduce useful approaches, but let the accepted result and total cost decide whether to continue. A successful within-team process does not depend on this second stage passing.

The ratio applies to this parser workload. Milliseconds saved on another service, or percentage gains on unrelated components, do not necessarily have the same business value. Compare workflows within each component; allocate budgets across components using explicit estimates of customer benefit or operating-cost savings.
